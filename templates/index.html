ℹ️ About this data & caveats

TRI data is self-reported annually by facilities above certain size and chemical-use thresholds. It undercounts — small emitters and non-covered industries aren't included. Releases being reported doesn't necessarily mean they're illegal or unsafe; it means the facility disclosed them as required by law. Quantities are pounds released per year. Source: EPA Toxics Release Inventory via Envirofacts.

ℹ️ About landfills & monitoring

Landfill monitoring (groundwater, air, leachate) is legally required but the results are generally not published online — they're available from EGLE by FOIA request. This layer shows facility locations, types, and regulatory status. Where a facility also reports toxic releases (TRI) or is a contaminated site (Superfund), that data is linked in its popup.

Coverage: EGLE's open data lists active / accepting licensed facilities. Closed, post-closure, and pre-regulation (unlined) landfills — often the bigger contamination risk — are not comprehensively mapped here; many appear in the Contamination sites overlay instead. Capacity/volume and monitoring results are not in the feed and are never guessed. Sources: Michigan EGLE Part 115 landfills & Part 111 hazardous-waste disposal facilities (Materials Management Open Data).

ℹ️ About golf courses & pesticide use

This layer shows where golf courses are located, not how much pesticide they apply. Michigan does not require public reporting of golf-course pesticide use, and industry records are not publicly available. The chemicals listed in each popup are those commonly used in turfgrass management generally (from turf IPM/BMP references), not records for any specific course. Public/municipally-owned courses are colored green — their records can more often be obtained by a FOIA request; most courses aren't tagged with an owner in OpenStreetMap and show grey. Note also that the app's agricultural pesticide layer (USGS EPest) excludes golf courses, lawns, and other non-agricultural uses entirely — so this land use is missing from those county totals. Locations are crowd-sourced (OpenStreetMap) and coverage may be incomplete or out of date.

ℹ️ About PFAS data

PFAS investigation in Michigan is ongoing — the site list grows as new contamination is found, so absence of a PFAS site does not mean absence of PFAS. An Area of Interest is an area under investigation where the source hasn't been determined; residential wells there may be affected. Public water supply results are shown as general hexbin areas rather than precise locations — EGLE withholds exact system locations by design to protect critical infrastructure. Concentrations are in parts per trillion (ppt); EPA's 2024 drinking-water limit for PFOA and PFOS is 4 ppt. Fish sampling links to MDHHS Eat Safe Fish guidance rather than inventing consumption advice. Nothing here is fabricated. Source: Michigan PFAS Action Response Team (MPART) / EGLE, live.

ℹ️ About storage tanks

Michigan regulates underground storage tanks under two different laws, kept distinct here: Part 211 (LARA) covers tanks that are merely licensed — a working gas station is not, by itself, a contaminated site — while Part 213 (EGLE) covers leaking tanks with a confirmed release needing corrective action. Red = open release, amber = closed/remediated, grey = licensed only. This covers known, registered tanks — unregistered or long-abandoned tanks, including most residential heating-oil tanks, are generally not included, so absence here doesn't mean none was ever present. Point locations vary in accuracy; some were placed by address matching, not GPS. Source: Michigan EGLE Remediation & Redevelopment Division; per-site detail in RIDE Mapper.

ℹ️ About the spraying-programs directory

This is a directory of known, organized spraying programs in Michigan and links to their official pages for current schedules. It is not a complete list of all spraying — private agricultural spraying and many local treatments are not included, and it does not publish specific spray dates. For the most current spray dates and notices in your area, contact your county or the official program page linked in each marker.

ℹ️ About the coal-ash directory

Under the federal CCR rule, coal-ash monitoring data is required to be public, but each utility posts it individually on its own website — there is no central database. So this layer links to those sources rather than aggregating live results. It aims to be complete: it mirrors EPA's list of Michigan coal combustion residuals sites. Coal ash contains arsenic, lead, lithium and other toxics; units may be a surface impoundment (ash pond) or a landfill, being closed by removal or cap-in-place. EPA's 2024 Legacy CCR Rule extended requirements to previously unregulated inactive impoundments. Some groundwater-contamination findings shown are contested between watchdog groups (Earthjustice / the Environmental Integrity Project) and the utilities — the popups attribute who found what and note the dispute.

        ℹ️ Important context — click to expand
        • Urban counties (Wayne / Oakland / Macomb / Genesee / Saginaw) have high asthma rates driven by air pollution, poverty, housing conditions, and industrial emissions — not agricultural pesticides.
        • Asthma and COPD have many causes: smoking, indoor allergens, air quality, genetics, occupational exposures, socioeconomic factors.
        • Pesticide applicators have direct occupational exposure risk; that differs from ambient community exposure.
        • Pesticide and respiratory data are annual and county-level. Monthly seasonal correlation isn't possible at the county grain.
        • Broader ICD-10 categories (J00-J06, J20-J22, J60-J70) use Michigan statewide baselines because no county-level breakdowns are published.
        • This tool is for exploratory visualization only — it does not constitute epidemiological evidence.

        ℹ️ Important context — click to expand
        • Cancer has long latency (10–30 years). Current rates may reflect exposures from decades ago, not today's application levels.
        • The pesticide data (through ~2019) and NCI cancer data (2018–2022) cover overlapping but not identical periods.
        • This is an ecological comparison — it compares county populations, not individuals. Individual exposure data would be needed for causal links (the ecological fallacy).
        • Major confounders: smoking, alcohol, obesity, occupational/industrial exposure, socioeconomic status, healthcare/screening access, age structure, and other contaminants (PFAS, industrial pollution).
        • Urban counties have different cancer profiles for reasons unrelated to agriculture — urban/rural stratification is essential.
        • Some agricultural counties may have lower overall cancer rates (younger, healthier, lower-smoking populations) — this does not mean pesticides are protective.
        • Data suppression in low-population counties (<16 cases) creates gaps that can bias the analysis.
        • This tool is for exploratory visualization only. It does not constitute epidemiological evidence of causation.

        Pesticide–cancer evidence

        Literature-based associations (IARC monographs + Agricultural Health Study). This is the scientific basis behind the compounds shown in the matrix — separate from the ecological correlations computed from Michigan county data.

        IARC groups: 1 = carcinogenic; 2A = probably; 2B = possibly; 3 = not classifiable. Evidence strength reflects epidemiological weight, not regulatory status.

        Welcome — start here

        The Michigan Pollution Map is a free tool for exploring Michigan's pollution, contamination, and community-health data on one map. It brings together pesticide use, industrial contamination (Superfund), toxic releases (TRI), water quality, and cancer and respiratory health so you can look for patterns and ask better questions.

        🗺️

        Explore the map

        Use Map layers (left) to color counties by pesticide use, cancer, respiratory health, contamination, or industrial toxic releases (TRI), and to stack overlays like water sites, factory markers, and wind. Hover anything with a ? for an explanation.

        📊

        Compare two things

        The Explore correlations tab lets you pick any two measures — say, herbicide use vs. a cancer rate — and see whether they rise and fall together across counties, explained in plain English.

        What the data is

        Everything comes from public sources (USGS, EPA, CDC, NCI, Michigan DNR/EGLE). Open Data sources any time to see each dataset, how current it is, and its limitations.

        About this project. The Michigan Pollution Map was built by Michael Tarbutton — an independent, non-commercial educational project that compiles publicly available data from USGS, EPA, CDC, NCI, USDA, and Michigan state agencies onto one map. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of them. It exists to make public environmental and health data easier to explore and to help people ask better-informed questions.

        Found an error or a missing site? Community groups, journalists, and residents often know about contamination the public data doesn't capture. Corrections and tips make this tool better — get in touch: mikeyt1719@gmail.com.

        Please read: This tool is for exploration and education only. Any patterns it shows are associations, not proof of cause — counties differ in age, income, industry, smoking, and many other ways that affect health. Nothing here is medical, legal, regulatory, or scientific advice. Do not use it to draw conclusions about any individual person, place, or product.

        Data sources

        All data is fetched live from the sources listed below. Where a source is unavailable or requires registration, it is marked accordingly and excluded from the map until configured. The Coverage column shows the date range each layer's data spans; Last updated shows when it was last refreshed.

        Data limitations: USGS EPest values are estimates derived from proprietary sale data and crop-extent models. Low/high bracket the uncertainty — see USGS Data Series 907 for methodology. County totals include only crop-protection use; lawn, golf, and aquatic non-agricultural use are not included.
