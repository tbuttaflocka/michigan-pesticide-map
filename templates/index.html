ℹ️ About this data & caveats

TRI data is self-reported annually by facilities above certain size and chemical-use thresholds. It undercounts — small emitters and non-covered industries aren't included. Releases being reported doesn't necessarily mean they're illegal or unsafe; it means the facility disclosed them as required by law. Quantities are pounds released per year. Source: EPA Toxics Release Inventory via Envirofacts.

ℹ️ About landfills & monitoring

Landfill monitoring (groundwater, air, leachate) is legally required but the results are generally not published online — they're available from EGLE by FOIA request. This layer shows facility locations, types, and regulatory status. Where a facility also reports toxic releases (TRI) or is a contaminated site (Superfund), that data is linked in its popup.

Coverage: EGLE's open data lists active / accepting licensed facilities. Closed, post-closure, and pre-regulation (unlined) landfills — often the bigger contamination risk — are not comprehensively mapped here; many appear in the Contamination sites overlay instead. Capacity/volume and monitoring results are not in the feed and are never guessed. Sources: Michigan EGLE Part 115 landfills & Part 111 hazardous-waste disposal facilities (Materials Management Open Data).

ℹ️ About EPA ECHO enforcement data

These are alleged violations reflecting determinations by EPA or state agencies when inspecting or reviewing self-reports — not final adjudications by a court or administrative body. Compliance status covers only the trailing 12 federal fiscal quarters (~3 years).

ECHO is a snapshot that lags the underlying source databases by up to three months; Safe Drinking Water Act data refreshes quarterly while the rest refresh weekly.

Absence of a violation is not evidence of compliance. 76,294 of 81,934 Michigan facilities (93%) have no recorded inspections — a facility showing no violations may simply never have been inspected. No data does not mean clean or compliant. EPA also notes national data is generally more complete for larger facilities and can be partial or absent for small ones.

Data quality prior to November 2000 has not been assessed by EPA — some penalty and inspection dates in this data predate that. Source: EPA Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO), All-Data REST service, Michigan.

ℹ️ About oil & gas wells & fracturing

EGLE's well data contains no hydraulic-fracturing flag and no fluid-volume field. These 92,577 wells are not labeled as fractured, and this app does not label them as such.

EGLE states that more than 12,000 Michigan wells have been hydraulically fractured over more than half a century — mostly shallow Antrim Shale wells from the late 1980s and early 1990s. Those wells are not identifiable as fractured in any machine-readable public dataset: they predate FracFocus and there is no frac field in the well data.

Michigan defines High Volume Hydraulic Fracturing (HVHF) as 100,000+ gallons of primary carrier fluid. Disclosure was mandatory for HVHF only from April 2015. FracFocus holds 31 Michigan disclosures covering 26 wells, 2011–2018, with none since 2016.

EGLE publishes its HVHF well list only as a static PDF map, not as data. The chemicals operators actually disclosed for Michigan HVHF wells are in this app's FracFocus popups — they include water, crystalline silica (quartz sand), hydrochloric acid, methanol, isopropanol, ethylene glycol, guar gum and ammonium persulfate (Source: this app's FracFocus disclosure records). But operators may withhold ingredient identities as trade secrets — 12.5% of Michigan ingredient records are masked, so the disclosed list is incomplete by design (masked entries are shown verbatim, never guessed, in the disclosure popups). Sources: EGLE GRMD / GeoWebFace (wells); FracFocus (chemical disclosures).

Well integrity over time. Plugging seals an abandoned well with cement plugs to isolate underground formations and keep oil, gas and brine from migrating up the wellbore into fresh groundwater (EPA). This layer holds 65,058 plugged and 214 orphan wells. Plugs are engineered to last, but peer-reviewed reviews of well abandonment document that cement can crack or de-bond from the casing over decades, and a minority of plugged wells have been found to leak — e.g. a Netherlands survey found 1 of 29 plugged wells leaking, and USGS studies wellbore deterioration from corrosive fluids, casing/cement delamination and seismicity. That is general engineering literature (Plug & abandonment review, ScienceDirect 2023; USGS), not a finding about any specific Michigan well. Orphan wells are those whose operator EGLE has determined is unknown, insolvent, or deceased; the state's Orphan Well Program (funded by an oil & gas severance tax under NREPA Part 616, plus federal Bipartisan Infrastructure Law money) plugs them (EGLE; U.S. Dept. of the Interior).

Produced water / brine. Antrim Shale gas is biogenic (generated by microbes), and its wells co-produce large volumes of formation water — one of the most water-productive gas plays (USGS). Published Michigan / Antrim studies report what that produced water contains: a sodium–chloride brine (also calcium and bromide) whose total dissolved solids average about 86,800 mg/L and reach ~116,000 mg/L (chloride ~22,000–141,000 mg/L); the trace metals strontium and barium; the radium isotopes radium-226 and radium-228; and dissolved organics including the organic acid acetate and hydrocarbons benzene, toluene and xylene. Sources: ACS Biogeochemistry of the Antrim Shale (2021) for salts and metals; EPA TENORM: Oil & Gas Production Wastes for radium; Antrim formation-water microbial studies (Frontiers in Microbiology, 2013) for organics. This brine is generally disposed of by reinjection into deep Class II wells (EPA; EGLE) — the 2,203 disposal/injection wells in this layer.

These constituents come from formation and regional studies — not from sampling at any individual well shown on this map. Presence in produced water is not evidence of release or exposure at any location.

Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S). EGLE records an H₂S concentration for wells because sour gas contains hydrogen sulfide — a colorless, flammable gas with a rotten-egg odor that is toxic and corrosive. It is especially dangerous because it rapidly deadens the sense of smell, so odor is an unreliable warning, and high concentrations can cause rapid unconsciousness and death (OSHA; NIOSH treats 100 ppm as immediately dangerous to life and health). Michigan's shallow Antrim biogenic gas is generally low in H₂S; higher concentrations are associated with deeper Niagaran reef gas. The stored value flags which wells need those precautions.

TENORM. Naturally occurring radioactive elements underground can concentrate in produced brine and in the mineral scale that builds up inside pipes and tanks — technologically enhanced naturally occurring radioactive material (TENORM). EPA names radium-226 and radium-228 in produced water, and lead-210 and polonium-210 in pipe and equipment scale (EPA TENORM: Oil & Gas Production Wastes; USGS FS-142-99). Michigan regulates disposal through EGLE's radiological program and PA 688 of 2018: produced water is generally reinjected, contaminated pipe scale is cleaned and drummed, and only Type II landfills meeting radium limits may accept TENORM (EGLE Materials Management).

No data does not mean no problem. This layer records wells — their locations, types and regulatory status — not their impacts. EGLE's well data does not measure groundwater, air or health, so the absence of a record here is not evidence that a well is leak-free or that no problem exists.

ℹ️ About power-plant emissions

EPA's Clean Air Markets program (CAMD) covers only fossil-combustion units subject to the Acid Rain Program, CSAPR or MATS. 256 of Michigan's 289 plants are not in it — including all three nuclear plants, the Ludington pumped-storage hydro plant, and all wind and solar. Absence of emissions data is not evidence of no emissions: nuclear, hydro and renewables simply don't emit SO₂/NOₓ from combustion, while small fossil units fall below the program's reporting thresholds.

The reported SO₂/NOₓ/CO₂ are CEMS stack measurements under 40 CFR Part 75 — measured, not modeled — this is the app's only measured air-emissions data. Part 75 substitutes conservative values when a monitor is offline, and some smaller units report calculated rather than CEMS values.

Mercury mass is NOT in this dataset — mercury is reported separately under MATS from 2015, so a zero here does not mean mercury data is absent. And only SO₂, NOₓ, CO₂, heat input and gross load are reported — no particulate matter, no VOCs, no hazardous air pollutants. Sources: EIA Form 860 (inventory); EPA CAMD / Clean Air Markets (emissions).

ℹ️ About air monitors & the coverage gap

These are measured ambient concentrations — what monitors actually recorded — from EPA's Air Quality System (AQS) AirData files. This is distinct from the power-plant layer (measured stack emissions, CEMS/CAMD) and the air-toxics layer (modeled risk); the three are never ranked or compared. Monitors are sited for specific objectives (population exposure, highest-concentration, source-oriented, background) at a defined measurement scale — a reading characterizes air at that monitor's scale and objective and does not represent unmonitored surrounding areas.

54 of Michigan's 83 counties have NO air monitor — including large ones like Saginaw, Jackson, Midland, Livingston, Calhoun and Van Buren. A county with no monitor is not a county with clean air — it is a county where no one is measuring. Turn on the county-coloring option “Air monitor coverage” to see which counties are measured and which are not.

Each reading is compared only to the current NAAQS it was actually measured against (never a mismatched averaging form), and exceedance counts are shown exactly as AQS reports them. Recent-year data may not be certified — the popup shows each reading's certification status; agencies have up to 6 months to report, and AirData refreshes twice a year. Source: EPA AQS (AirData annual summaries + monitor list).

ℹ️ About golf courses & pesticide use

This layer shows where golf courses are located, not how much pesticide they apply. Michigan does not require public reporting of golf-course pesticide use, and industry records are not publicly available. The chemicals listed in each popup are those commonly used in turfgrass management generally (from turf IPM/BMP references), not records for any specific course. Public/municipally-owned courses are colored green — their records can more often be obtained by a FOIA request; most courses aren't tagged with an owner in OpenStreetMap and show grey. Note also that the app's agricultural pesticide layer (USGS EPest) excludes golf courses, lawns, and other non-agricultural uses entirely — so this land use is missing from those county totals. Locations are crowd-sourced (OpenStreetMap) and coverage may be incomplete or out of date.

ℹ️ About PFAS data

PFAS investigation in Michigan is ongoing — the site list grows as new contamination is found, so absence of a PFAS site does not mean absence of PFAS. An Area of Interest is an area under investigation where the source hasn't been determined; residential wells there may be affected. Public water supply results are shown as general hexbin areas rather than precise locations — EGLE withholds exact system locations by design to protect critical infrastructure. Concentrations are in parts per trillion (ppt); EPA's 2024 drinking-water limit for PFOA and PFOS is 4 ppt. Fish sampling links to MDHHS Eat Safe Fish guidance rather than inventing consumption advice. Nothing here is fabricated. Source: Michigan PFAS Action Response Team (MPART) / EGLE, live.

ℹ️ About storage tanks

Michigan regulates underground storage tanks under two different laws, kept distinct here: Part 211 (LARA) covers tanks that are merely licensed — a working gas station is not, by itself, a contaminated site — while Part 213 (EGLE) covers leaking tanks with a confirmed release needing corrective action. Red = open release, amber = closed/remediated, grey = licensed only. This covers known, registered tanks — unregistered or long-abandoned tanks, including most residential heating-oil tanks, are generally not included, so absence here doesn't mean none was ever present. Point locations vary in accuracy; some were placed by address matching, not GPS. Source: Michigan EGLE Remediation & Redevelopment Division; per-site detail in RIDE Mapper.

ℹ️ About the spraying-programs directory

This is a directory of known, organized spraying programs in Michigan and links to their official pages for current schedules. It is not a complete list of all spraying — private agricultural spraying and many local treatments are not included, and it does not publish specific spray dates. For the most current spray dates and notices in your area, contact your county or the official program page linked in each marker.

ℹ️ About the coal-ash directory

Under the federal CCR rule, coal-ash monitoring data is required to be public, but each utility posts it individually on its own website — there is no central database. So this layer links to those sources rather than aggregating live results. It aims to be complete: it mirrors EPA's list of Michigan coal combustion residuals sites. Coal ash contains arsenic, lead, lithium and other toxics; units may be a surface impoundment (ash pond) or a landfill, being closed by removal or cap-in-place. EPA's 2024 Legacy CCR Rule extended requirements to previously unregulated inactive impoundments. Some groundwater-contamination findings shown are contested between watchdog groups (Earthjustice / the Environmental Integrity Project) and the utilities — the popups attribute who found what and note the dispute.

ℹ️ About the Crop Map (CDL)

Why it's here. The pesticide-use figures elsewhere in this app are county-level estimates — they don't show where inside a county the applications actually happen. The crop map shows where cropland actually is, so you can see which parts of a county are farmed and what is growing near a given location.

What it is. The USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) is a satellite-derived classification, not a field survey or a record of what was actually planted or sprayed. It shows predicted crop cover for the selected year, and its per-crop accuracy varies — major commodities like corn and soybeans classify more reliably than minor crops. Treat it as context, not ground truth. For the full legend and per-crop accuracy see the official page: USDA NASS — Cropland Data Layer. Source: USDA NASS Cropland Data Layer.

        Welcome — start here

        The Michigan Pollution Map is a free tool for exploring Michigan's pollution, contamination, and exposure data on one map. It brings together pesticide use, industrial contamination (Superfund), toxic releases (TRI), water quality, and more so you can look for patterns and ask better questions.

        🗺️

        Explore the map

        Use Map layers (left) to color counties by pesticide use, contamination, or industrial toxic releases (TRI), and to stack overlays like water sites, factory markers, and wind. Hover anything with a ? for an explanation.

        What the data is

        Everything comes from public sources (USGS, EPA, Michigan DNR/EGLE). Open Data sources any time to see each dataset, how current it is, and its limitations.

        About this project. The Michigan Pollution Map was built by Michael Tarbutton — an independent, non-commercial educational project that compiles publicly available data from USGS, EPA, USDA, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), and Michigan state agencies (EGLE, MDHHS), among others, onto one map. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of them. It exists to make public environmental data easier to explore and to help people ask better-informed questions.

        Found an error or a missing site? Community groups, journalists, and residents often know about contamination the public data doesn't capture. Corrections and tips make this tool better — get in touch: mikeyt1719@gmail.com.

        Please read. This tool is for exploration and education only — not medical, legal, regulatory, or scientific advice.

        No data does not mean clean or safe. Many places have never been tested, sampled, or inspected; an area with no records may simply be unmonitored.

        Sources differ. The data comes from many sources with different methods, thresholds, and time periods — check each layer's About text before drawing conclusions.

        Data sources

        All data is fetched live from the sources listed below. Where a source is unavailable or requires registration, it is marked accordingly and excluded from the map until configured. The Coverage column shows the date range each layer's data spans; Last updated shows when it was last refreshed.

        Data limitations: USGS EPest values are estimates derived from proprietary sale data and crop-extent models. Low/high bracket the uncertainty — see USGS Data Series 907 for methodology. County totals include only crop-protection use; lawn, golf, and aquatic non-agricultural use are not included.
